The quiet economics of sleep

Across the last two decades, researchers have moved sleep from the margins of public-health debate to its centre. Where fatigue was once treated as a personal failing, it is now measured as an economic variable: hours lost, accidents caused, productivity quietly drained.

A 2019 study across five industrial economies estimated that insufficient sleep costs between 1.5 and 3 per cent of annual GDP. The mechanism is unglamorous — slower decisions, more errors, higher absenteeism — but the aggregate is large enough that several governments now fund sleep-education programmes in secondary schools.

Critics argue the figures rest on self-reported data and that framing rest as an economic input risks turning recovery into another performance target. Even so, the shift has changed how workplaces are designed: later start times, dimmer evening lighting, and shift patterns rebuilt around circadian rhythm rather than convenience.
